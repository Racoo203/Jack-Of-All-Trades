## Definition
Monte Carlo (MC) methods learn value functions and optimal policies purely from **experience**: sampled sequences of states, actions, and rewards from actual or simulated interaction with an environment, requiring no knowledge of the dynamics $p(s',r\mid s,a)$ ([[Model-free & Model-based|model-free]]). MC methods require **episodic** tasks (see [[Infinite & Finite Time Horizon]]), since they need a completed episode to compute a well-defined return $G_t$.

## Properties
### First-visit vs. every-visit prediction
To estimate $v_\pi(s)$ from episodes generated under $\pi$: every time $s$ occurs in an episode is a **visit** to $s$ (it may be visited multiple times per episode). The **first-visit MC** method averages the returns following only the *first* visit to $s$ in each episode; the **every-visit MC** method averages returns following *every* visit. Both converge asymptotically to $v_\pi(s)$ (by the Law of Large Numbers, as the visit count $N(s)\to\infty$), but they generally give **different estimates from the same finite data** — a recurring MA338 exam question asks for both from the same short episode.

### Incremental MC updates
Analogous to bandit updates (see [[Multi-armed bandits]]), after each episode, for every visited $S_t$ with observed return $G_t$:
$$N(S_t)\leftarrow N(S_t)+1,\qquad V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\frac{1}{N(S_t)}[G_t-V(S_t)],$$
or, for non-stationary problems, with constant step size $\alpha$: $V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha[G_t-V(S_t)]$.

### Why MC doesn't bootstrap
Each state's estimate is built **only** from actual sampled returns — it never depends on the *current estimate* of another state's value, unlike [[Dynamic programming|DP]]. This means MC estimates for different states are independent, which is advantageous for parallelisation, but forfeits the ability to propagate information between interconnected states the way bootstrapping does. This is the defining structural contrast with [[Temporal-Difference Learning]], which *does* bootstrap.

### Action-value prediction
Policy evaluation for $q_\pi(s,a)$ follows the same visit-counting logic, now over state-*action* pairs. A subtlety: if $\pi$ is deterministic, following it generates returns for only *one* action per state — the other actions are never visited, and their estimates never improve. This is the **maintaining exploration** problem, and is why MC control requires either exploring starts or [[On-policy & Off-policy|soft policies]].

### On-policy MC Control ($\epsilon$-soft)
Generalised Policy Iteration applies here too: $\pi_0\to_E q_{\pi_0}\to_I \pi_1 \to_E q_{\pi_1}\cdots\to \pi^*\to_E q_{\pi^*}$. Under the (unrealistic) assumptions of infinite episodes and exploring starts, MC computes each $q_{\pi_k}$ exactly and policy improvement (greedy w.r.t. $q_{\pi_k}$) uniformly improves the policy (Policy Improvement Theorem). Removing the infinite-episodes assumption is straightforward (as in DP); removing exploring starts requires **soft policies**: $\pi(a\mid s)>0$ for all $s,a$ — see [[On-policy & Off-policy]] for the $\epsilon$-soft form and full pseudocode.

### Off-policy MC Control via Importance Sampling
To learn about a target policy $\pi$ from experience generated by a different behaviour policy $b$, the **coverage assumption** must hold: $\pi(a\mid s)>0 \implies b(a\mid s)>0$. The [[On-policy & Off-policy|importance-sampling ratio]] $\rho_{t:T-1}=\prod_{k=t}^{T-1}\pi(A_k\mid S_k)/b(A_k\mid S_k)$ reweights behaviour-policy returns to have the correct target-policy expectation: $v_\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[\rho_{t:T-1}G_t\mid S_t=s]$. In practice, **Weighted Importance Sampling** is used for numerical stability, updated incrementally with weights $W_n$ and cumulative sum $C_n$:
$$V_{n+1}=V_n+\frac{W_n}{C_n}[G_n-V_n],\qquad C_{n+1}=C_n+W_{n+1},\ C_0=0.$$
Off-policy MC control (GPI + WIS) lets $\pi$ be fully greedy/deterministic (a natural target) while $b$ remains soft (a natural explorer), terminating the return-accumulation loop early once a taken action deviates from the greedy target policy.

## Examples
Blackjack (Lecture 4): the rules give complete knowledge of the environment, but deriving $p(s',r\mid s,a)$ in closed form is complex (requires enumerating remaining-deck probabilities), whereas sampling games is trivial — a canonical illustration of why MC can be preferable even under full knowledge of the dynamics.
