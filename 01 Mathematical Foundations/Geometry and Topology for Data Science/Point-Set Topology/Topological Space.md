## Definition
Let $X$ be a set. A **topology** on $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of $X$ (called **open sets**) such that:

1. $\emptyset, X \in \mathcal{T}$.
2. If $U_1,\dots,U_n \in \mathcal{T}$, then $\bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i \in \mathcal{T}$ (finite intersections of open sets are open).
3. If $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I} \subseteq \mathcal{T}$, then $\bigcup_{\alpha\in I} U_\alpha \in \mathcal{T}$ (arbitrary unions of open sets are open).

The pair $(X,\mathcal{T})$ is a **topological space**. A topology models the "shape" of $X$ without reference to any [[Metric Space|metric]] or notion of distance.

## Properties
### Basic examples of topologies
- **Trivial (indiscrete) topology**: $\mathcal T = \{\emptyset, X\}$.
- **Discrete topology**: $\mathcal T = \mathcal P(X)$, the collection of *all* subsets of $X$.
- **Finite-complement (cofinite) topology**: $\mathcal T = \{\emptyset, X\} \cup \{A \subseteq X : X\setminus A \text{ is finite}\}$.
- **Product topology**: given $(X,\mathcal T_X)$ and $(Y,\mathcal T_Y)$, the open sets of $X\times Y$ are unions of "rectangles" $U\times V$ with $U\in\mathcal T_X$, $V \in \mathcal T_Y$.

The choice of topology depends on the problem: the trivial topology models $X$ as if it were a single point (no distinctions between subsets), while the discrete topology models $X$ as a "cloud" of completely separated points.

### Basis for a topology
A collection $\mathcal B$ of subsets of $X$ is a **basis** for a topology if (i) every $x\in X$ lies in some $B \in \mathcal B$, and (ii) whenever $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$ for $B_1,B_2\in\mathcal B$, there is $B_3\in\mathcal B$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1\cap B_2$. Given such a $\mathcal B$, declaring $U$ open iff every $x\in U$ has some $B\in\mathcal B$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$ defines a topology *generated by* $\mathcal B$. The open intervals of $\mathbb R$ form a basis for the **standard topology** on $\mathbb R$.

## Examples
Beyond metric-induced topologies, some important spaces in data science — e.g. the space of all probability measures on a set — are naturally topological but not metric spaces, which is exactly why the topological (rather than purely metric) machinery matters.
